
Slopes are typically trapezoidal piles of soil that do some bear some kind of load. An example of this is a simple dam. Slopes can suffer **overtopping**[^1] 

[^1]: When water spills over the top of the dam

### 1.1 Shear stress

Problems that arise from slope arise when the soil slips or slides when a load is applied on it (or when the slope cannot support itself. ![[Pasted image 20260122143645.png]]
The only thing keeping the soil from sliding is its **Shear Strength** we will be working with factor of safety when it comes to shear strength, which can be calculated by
$$
\text{F.S.}=\frac{\tau_{f}}{\tau}=\frac{\text{strength}}{\text{stress}}
$$
$\tau_{f}$ is the shear stress of the soil, while $\tau$ is the shear stress that is being applied onto the soil. A factor of safety that dips below 1 means that the soil will fail, 

Shear Stress $\tau=c'+\sigma'\tan \phi'=c'+(\sigma+u)\tan \phi'$ 

![[Pasted image 20260122144755.png]]

For coarse grained materials, as you construct the slope, the load will transfer onto the soil accordingly.

For fine grained materials, the soil may not feel the load immediately, this is due to the pore pressure[^3] that is in the soil. The water will slowly leave the soil, and this changes the soil's strength. This takes time, which is why we have staged construction[^2]. The change in pore pressure can be described as $\Delta \sigma=\Delta \sigma'+\Delta u$

[^2]: Construction that takes place in stages, often with long gaps before construction is continued
[^3]: pore pressure is strength from the water in the clay or silt
