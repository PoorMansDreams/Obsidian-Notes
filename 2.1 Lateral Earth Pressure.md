At normal conditions, the Lateral Earth Pressure will **always** be a fraction of the **vertical earth pressure** 
in short, 
$$
\sigma_{h}=K\times \sigma_{v}
$$
h is horizontal, while v is vertical. We multiply the pressure by the vertical soil by K, which will give us the horizontal pressure. 
A smaller unstable soil wedge means a smaller K,

Equivalent Fluid Density: $\gamma_{EF}=K\times \gamma$
Vertical Earth Pressure: $\sigma_{v}=\gamma \times z$
Lateral Earth Pressure: $\sigma_{h}=K\times \sigma_{v}=K\times \gamma \times z=\gamma_{EF}\times z$

If the soil is sloped, we cant use Rankine Directly, but we can make a few adjustments to be able to use it. 
$$
\begin{align*} K_a = \cos \alpha \frac{\cos \alpha - \sqrt{\cos^2 \alpha - \cos^2 \phi'}}{\cos \alpha + \sqrt{\cos^2 \alpha - \cos^2 \phi'}} \\[2ex] K_p = \cos \alpha \frac{\cos \alpha + \sqrt{\cos^2 \alpha - \cos^2 \phi'}}{\cos \alpha - \sqrt{\cos^2 \alpha - \cos^2 \phi'}} \end{align*}
$$

**Coulomb's Earth Pressure Theory** is less conservative, and is generally a bit more acurate.
* Equilibrium of soil wedge
* Can account for wall interface friction
* Can account for battered walls backs
* Can account for sloped grounds
