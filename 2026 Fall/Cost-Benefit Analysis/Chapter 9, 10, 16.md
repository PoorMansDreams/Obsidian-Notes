
### 9.1 Interventions in Markets
- **Free** **perfect markets**
	- Supply and demand should all lead to efficient allocation of resources
	- social marginal benefits equal social a marginal cost
	- Market failures or imperfections may call for government interventions
- **Government Intervention**
	- Directs the provisions of goods and services
		- Essentia goods and services are provided by the government
		- Like healthcare, education, police, and national defense
	- Legislation and regulation
		- Governments can prohibit and intervene
		- This might be a good idea because having a complete free market may have undesirable effects on everyone. 
- **Subsidies and taxes**
	- Carrot and stick policies
	- Taxes act as the stick by discouraging undesirable activities
		- Tax on pollution, cigarettes, petrol, and alcohol
	- Subsidies act as carrot by encouraging desirable policies
		- Positive externality-social marginal benefits i great than the private marginal benefit
		- making the product cheaper and encouraging greater consumption. 

### 9.2 Examples of Government Intervention in the Market System

- Regulating monopoly and competitive behavior
	- Intervene to protect consumers from anti-competitive market practices
	- When only a few firms dominate the market, there is less incentive for competition which leads to higher prices, lower quality services, and less investment in research and development
	- Over time leads to less innovation and higher cots for customers which is "X inefficiency"
	- Examples of anti-Competitive behavior:
		- Colluding and price fixing where businesses work together as a cartel instead of competing
		- Predatory pricing (price wars)

### 9.3 Regulating UK Mergers and Takeovers

### Creating a Market in Pollution
- Government can also intervene to deal with pollution
	- Pollution tax so that firms face more of the social cost of their activities.
	- direct regulation, where government establish legal limits on the amount of pollution firms can produce
	- In short, a firm that may produce much less pollution can use their extra permits for pollution, and sell their permit for pollution to another firm that may not be able to produce less pollution

### The Coase Theorem
- The Coase Theorem states that private parties can resolve property rights disputes and market externalities efficiently through direct bargaining, as long as transaction costs are low and property rights are clearly defined

### Min and Max Prices
- Price Control
	- Minimum - cannot fall below a certain level (wages)
	- Maximum - prices cannot rise to a certain level (rent)

### Price Stabilization
- Government intervene to stabilize the market
	- The government may do this by buying or selling large amounts of stock to change the supply, essentially going up and down the supply-demand curve (Buy when theres excess, Sell when theres scarcity)

### Privatization
- Privatization occurs when resources, services, or ownership are transferred form the public sector to the private sector
- forms of privatization:
	- Denationalization 
		- Sells the whole market
	- Contracting out
		- Government shares responsibility with the private sector 
	- Selling public sector assets
	- Selling government shares in private sector businesses
	- Deregulation
	- Private finance initiative (PFI)

### Reasons to Privatize
- Finance
	- More money comes when the government sells to the private
	- Government financing may be delayed, so it may be more efficient to give it to a private sector to complete faster
- Efficiency
	- Private business have the incentive to finish it because the government basically pays them
- Competition
	- Since these sectors are private, they WANT these public sector jobs

### Problems of Privatization
- May create private monopolies
- Private firms may seek private objectives instead of social objectives (may choose what is more profitable)
- If the public sector industries were making profits, then by privatizing them, the government loses this income
- Industry may fire and cut workers to better efficiency. 

### Problems with Government Intervention
- Information problems
	- May not capture what the people actually want
- Shortages and surpluses
- Lack of incentives
	- May reduce product effectiveness 
- Regulatory capture
- Valuation problems
- Bureaucracy

### Short Run and Law of Diminishing Returns
- Short run
	- Period where at least one factor of production is fixed (Office Space, Recruiting wanted staff, missing equipment)
- Fixed vs Variable Cost
	- Fixed costs don't change regardless of output (rent, interest from loan)
	- Variable costs can change and vary with output (materials, labor)
- Law of Diminishing Returns
	- As you add more of a variable factor such as (workers) into a already existing fixed setup, the extra output each new unit adds eventually shrinks (so much extra benefits something can bring, but fixed factor creates the constraint) Usually only applied to short run only 
		- Example: You're a business owner operating in an office, you try to increase efficiency but you don't more equipment. You hire more workers, but there's not enough computers to accommodate the workers. (too many cooks)
$$
\text{Marginal Cost}= \frac{\text{Change in the total cost}}{\text{Change in Output}}
$$
- More productive worker makes each extra unit cheaper, Diminishing returns can make it more expensive 
- Minimize how much the firm needs to spend, but maximum how much it outputs 

### Short Run Average Cost and Connection between Marginal and Average Cost

- Short Run Average Cost (SRAC)
	- The cost of producitng one unit of output in the short run (usually looks like a U shaped Curve) Given by the equation:
$$
 \text{Average Cost (AC)}= \text{Average Fixed Cost (AFC)} + \text{Average Variable Cost (AVC)}
$$
- If marginal cost is above average costs, it pulls average up, if marginal cost is below average cost it pulls average down
- Hence why marginal cost always crosses average cost

### Long Run Costs
- What is long run costs?
	- No longer fixed variables when determining the unit price
		- Find lowest total unit cost
	- Internal economies of scale
		- Quantity ($\uparrow$) = average cost per unit ($\downarrow$)

### Types of internal Economies of Scale
- Technical economies
	- More efficient machines
- Indivisibilities
	- Fixed machine sizes for different scales of the business
- Specialization and division of labor
	- Specialized roles to increase efficiency
	- How employees specialize in certain roles. 

### Return to Scale
- Increase return to scale
	- 2x input 3x output
- Decreasing return to scale
	- 3x input 2x output
- Constant Return to scale
	- 2x input 2x output 

### Minimum Efficient Scale
- The smallest amount a firm can produce while achieving the lowest possible long-run average cost
- Long run average cost
	- Cost of a product based on internal & external factors
- If the Minimum Efficient Scale (EMS) is small relative to total indsutyr demand
	- Firms operate efficiency
	- Market is competitive
- If the MES is large relative to total industry demand
	- Only few firms can survive
	- Market is most likely concentrated

### The Labor Market
- Wages aren't set by how "important" a job is but rather by supply and demand 
- Same logic as pricing any good or services; here the "good" is a person's time and skills, and the "price is the wage.
- Every profession is its own separate labor market, each with its own supply and demand

### Supply of Labor
- Supply: how many people are willing able to work in a profession at a given wge
- Long/difficult training periods restrict supply leading to shortages
- Higher wages pull in more people over time
- Size of the overall labor pool shifts how much supply is available 

### Wage Elasticity of Supply of Labor
- Measures how quickly workers respond to better pay by entering a job/industry
- Geographic mobility: moving costs, kids' schooling, family ties make relocating for a job difficult
- Occupational Mobility: swithincg carreers erquires retrainig/qualificatiosn
- The harder it is to move or retain, the less responsive labor supply is to wage changes

### Demand For Labor
- Demand = number of workers a firm is willing/able to employ at a given wage
- MPRL (marginal revenue product of labor) = extra revenue a firm earns from hiring one more worker
- Higher wages $\to$ fewer workers demanded
- Better management and workers training raise MRPL, supporting higher wages 

### Wage Elasticity of Demand for Labor
- Measures how much the quantity of labor demanded changes when wages change 
- Ease of substituting machines for workers
- Price elasticity of demand for the firm's product
- Wages as a share of total costs - labor intensive vs capital-intensive industries.

# A Firm's Decision to Hire Employees
- IN a perfectly competitive market, each firm is only a small piece of an industry
- Number of employees a single firm chooses to hire does not affect overall demand for labor
- **Wage Taker** - when a company/firm must accept the market wage determined by the industry
- Firms hire until the marginal cost is at equilibrium with the marginal revenue

### Wage Differentials in a Perfect Labor Market
- In a perfect labor market, all workers would be paid the same
- If wages are higher in one industry, people will move over to the higher paying job
- Increase in supply of workers will result in lower wage
- Less people in lower-paying industry results in higher wage
- Wages equal out 

### Why Wage Differentials Exist
- Moving between industries/jobs is not so simple in reality
- Certain jobs might not be appealing to certain people despite being higher paying
- Research awareness about a job might not reach qualified people.

### Monopsony
- In some markets, large firms dominate hiring and can alter equilibirum wages themselves
- Monopsony- when a single/small group of firms hold power over the market as the primary purchaser of goods/services
- Monopsonies = lower wages 

### Marginal revenue product model
- The Marginal revenue product (MRP) Model is a theory that measures how much extra input translates to extra revenue for a business (Ex: measures how measuring a worker may increase revenue)

### Marginal Revenue Product Model and Limitations
- Some fields are more difficult to measure productivity of each employee
- Some employee output has no market value
- There are millions of labor markets 
	- Ex: how do you gauge how well a security worker improves?
