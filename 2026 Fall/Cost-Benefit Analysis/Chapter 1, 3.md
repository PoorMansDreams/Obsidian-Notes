# Chapter 1
### What is Economics?


- Economics are limited while wants are unlimited
- every decision means giving something up
- decision depends on priorities costs and benefits

For Individuals, time and income are limited
For Businesses, Labor and capital
For Governments, public services (funding/budget)

This is called **Scarcity**

We want to find which one has the greatest benefit and has the least amount of sacrifice. 

### Economic Resources

**Factors of Production**

* Land
	* Natural Resources provided by nature
		* e.g. soil, water, oil
* Labor
	* Human physical and mental effort used to produce goods and services
		* e.g. engineers
* Capital
	* Man-made resources used to produce other goods and services 
		* e.g. machines, buildings, transportation
* Entrepreneurship
	* Creating ideas, combing resources, managing people, and taking risks
		* e.g. starting and managing a small business 

### Limited Resources Require Decisions

* The production of goods and services depend on more than what people want
* Limited resources cause economies to choose what to prioritize
* Every economy must answer the three fundamental questions 

Questions to ask: Who will benefit from these productions? Who is getting these goods? How will companies price items? 

### Opportunity Cost

* **Opportunity Cost** measures the sacrifice given up in the next best alternative
* Engineering projects are evaluated by cost, quality, and completion time
* Prioritizing two of these objectives will result in compromising the third 

Good, Fast, Cheap; you can have two but never all three.

### Modeling and Analysis of Markets and Economies

* What is an Economic Model?
	* Economies and markets can be simulated using a model, to predict how they will behave in the future
	* Models operate on several assumption, simplifying and/or omitting negligible data (not unlike scientific models)
* How Accurate are they?

### Micro vs Macroeconomics
- Microeconomics studies individual choices and specific markets, while macroeconomics examines the entire economy on a national or global scale

### Margins
* Something is marginal when it is considered **additional**, typically referring to costs and benefits
* Balancing marginal costs and what people want is important

### Nominal vs Real Values
* Nominal values do not consider inflation, only current values
	* Example: your wage increase over time is nominal
	* Nominal values are not always an accurate indication of purchasing power 
* Real Values
	* Real values are adjusted for inflation and consider future values
	* Example: Your wage increase over time minus the increase in inflation is real
	* Real values are more indicative of purchasing power.

### Demands & Markets
- In a free-market economy, supply and demand help determine:
	- What goods and services are produced and who can afford them
	- How resources such as labor and machinery are used
	- Prices and wages adjust when market conditions change, reallocating resources across industries.
	- Product markets and factor markets are connected:
	- Higher demand for a product increases demand for the resources needed to produce it and vice versa. 
		- Changes in demand help the economy moves. 

### Demand Curve

* A demand curve normally slopes downward:
* At a higher price, a smaller quantity is demanded.
* At a lower price, a larger quantity is demanded
* A linear demand curve can be drawn,
	* $Q=a-bP$
	* Q = Quantity Demanded
	* P = Price
	* a = quantity demanded when the price is zero 
	* b = responsiveness of quantity to price 

### Marginal and Total Utility
* **Marginal Utility** measures the extra utility or satisfaction from consuming an additional unit of a product
* **Total utility** is the total satisfaction from the consumption of a product.
	* As a person consumes addition units, the person will slowly become less and less satisfied, people will only be willing to purchase more if the price falls

### Movement Along the Demand Curve
- **Extension of demand:**
	- Caused by a fall in price
	- Results in an increase in quantity demanded
	- shown as a downward movement along the demand curve
- **Contraction of demand**
	- Caused by a rise in price
	- Results in a decrease in quantity demanded
	- Shown as a upward movement along the demand curve. 
Increase in coffee price may shift tea's demand curve

### Income and Substitution Effects
* **Substitution effect** is when a product's price falls, it becomes relatively cheaper than alternatives, encouraging consumers to switch towards it and increasing quantity demanded.
* **Income effect** is when a lower price increases consumer's real income or purchasing power, allowing them to buy more. 
### Forces That Can Change Demand Curve (At Every Price)
- **Income**
	- Normal goods: income up = demand up
	- Inferior goods, income up = demand down
- **Number of Buyers**
	- Population growth or a larger group usually shifts market demand right 
- **Complements**
	- Price of a complement up = demand for this product usually falls
- **Expectations**
	- If buyers expect future prices to rise or shortages to occur, they buy more right now
- **Special Influences
	- External conditions like weather, seasonal changes, or credit availability can also act as the sixth variable depending on the specific economic model.
- **Tastes and Preferences** 
	- A shift in popularity or fashion trends (such as advertising or health awareness) makes people want more or less of a product at every price


### Individual Demand vs. Market Demand
- **Individual demand**
	- Quantity one consumer is willing and able to buy at each price
- **Market demand**
	- total demand from all consumers

Market demand is found by adding individual quantities at each price. 

### Capital Goods vs Consumer Goods
- Capital Goods are good used to make other goods, like fabric (for cloths)
- Consumer Goods are goods that are sold straight 

### Demographics Change Demand 
- How population changes affect demand
	- More people living alone increases demand for smaller packages and single serving products
	- An aging population increases demand for healthcare poroducts, dining and entertainment
	- Rising middle-class populations increase spending on recreation, services, and healthcare
	- Urbanization concentrates demand in growing cities. 

### Derived Demand
- Derived demand is demand that exists because another product or service is demanded 
	- Demands for car windshield comes from demand for cars
	- demand for construction workers come from demand for construction projects
	- demand for steel and concrete comes from demand for buildings and infrastructure
	- demand for land to grow rapeseeds comes from demand for rapeseed oil. 

### Joint Demand
- What is Joint Demand?
	- Joint demand occurs when the same product is demanded by several different sectors or for several different purposes. 
		- Ex: Demand for Gold
			- Jewelry
			- Investment
			- Industry
