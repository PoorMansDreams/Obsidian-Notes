Due to accumulation of organic matter and silts, lakes undergo a natural aging process known as **eutrophication**

Young lakes with low nutrient content and low biological productivity are called **oligotrophic**

over time because of the increased biological productivity, lakes become **eutrophic**

* The main issue with this is the depletion of dissolved oxygen, especially at the bottom of the lake, Anaerobic condition causes the production of H2S and toxic metals going around, Lakes and ponds are vulnerable to this and acid rains, accumulating wastes.

Controlling factors of Eutrophication
* Sunlight
* Photosynthesis
* Transparency of water
* Level of Eutrophication

The littoral zone is close to the shore, the limnetic zone is basically the middle/deep end of the water. Euphotic zone is the top/surface of the water. A a lot of sunlight goes through this zone, so a lot of productivity happens here. Profundal zone a lot of H2S forms here since not as much light goes through. Benthic zone is at the very bottom, where organic matter and nasty stuff settles down. 

**Light Water** is water that is warmer or colder than 4$^\circ$C Accumulates on the lake surface, Denser water at the bottom. **Thermal Stratification** prevents certain levels of water from mixing. 

* ***Epilimnion** - Top most part, completely mixed, most uniform texture, 1-20m thick.
* ***Thermocline (Metalimnion)** - Transition layer, thinner, rapid changes in temp
* **Hypolimnion** - Bottom layer, coldest layer.
Note that Stratification exists in Winter and Summer, but disappears during Fall and Spring

Our groundwater can be contaminated via many ways;
- Leaking underground storage tanks
- industrial waste
- home septic tanks
- municipal and industrial landfills
- accidental chem spills
- illegal dumping
- excessive use of agricultural chemicals

##### Drawing a Hydraulic Gradient
1. Draw a line between the two wells with the highest (h3) and lowest head (h1) and divide that line into equal intervals. Identify the location on the line where the head is equal to the head of the third (intermediate head, h2) well. 
2. Draw a line between the intermediate-head well and the spot on the above line that corresponds to the head at the intermediate well. This is an equipotential line, meaning that the head anywhere along the line should be approximately constant (i.e., h2). 
3. Draw a line perpendicular to the equipotential line through the well with the lowest (or highest) head. This is a flow line (streamline), which means groundwater flow is in a direction parallel to this line. 4. Determine the gradient as the difference in head between the head on the equipotential and the head at the lowest (or highest) well, divided by the distance from the equipotential line to that well (L).
