These sheets have giant corrugations. They often have these hooks that interlink as seen in the middle bottom picture. (they are slid in top down). Sometimes, walls don't need corrugation because the bending moment caused by the soil isn't high. 

The green wall supports the soil behind it, but also supports the concrete abutment above it. This makes it a deep foundation type of thing. The green is most likely a corrosion resistant layer on the steel.

These sheet piles are hammered (driven) down. They can be used for both cut and fill. For example, we drink a wall down into the ground, then excavate one side of the wall.

Soldier pile walls are made of vertical H-beams. With wood or concrete "laggings" (planks), that are put in-between the H-beams. As we excavate, the laggings will drop down, and we simply add more at the top to fall down. 

The giant metal things sticking out of the walls are called compression props.

![[Pasted image 20260414143210.png]]

These excavation machines are made to cut very deep into the ground (uses drilling mud). The drilling mud (bentonite+water) has high density, and actually supports the soil. If we let the drilling mud set after drilling the hole. It will turn into a slurry wall. But we don't want that. 

We drill a hole with drilling mud, then the hole will be full of drilling mud.
What we do is we use a tremie (long funnel) to pour concrete down into the hole, WHILE the drilling mud is still in the hole. The concrete is heavier than the drilling mud, so the mud will float to the top, while the concrete fills the bottom. Eventually the concrete will replace the drilling mud. Let it set, and it'll become a strong reinforced concrete wall. (There is some rebar hung in the concrete while it's being poured)

These are concrete cylinder. For secant piles, we construct the unsupported ones first. Then we drill into them to create the new cylinders in between. The "complete" cylinders are done second. The "incomplete" ones are done first. (The incomplete ones are drilled into for the complete ones).
Some of these walls are water tight.

All walls could be used to support other walls. Rakers are angled beams that push against the wall by using the ground as support. Raker's main downside is that they interfere with activities on site. They limit mobility of machinery and everything.

Struts \(bottom right) 

![[Pasted image 20260414145542.png]]

Anchored struts don't protrude into the excavated area, so they don't get in the way. However, the anchor may enter a zone we cannot touch, (issues with right of way). We also will need to excavate the side we aren't working on. 

The way these work, is that the plate experiences passive earth pressure (soil pushes on the plate to the right). The active earth pressure (pushing to the left) will push on the wall. There is a tension cable that connects the plate and the wall. In short, we are using the passive earth pressure to fight against the active earth pressure

![[Pasted image 20260414150631.png]]

These work with most types of walls. As we start excavating on one side of the wall, we can dig a angled hole, fill it with grout connected to a tension cable. Continue excavating, then keep repeating until we reach our desired depth. It doesn't need to be in the passive area like the deadman do. These just need to be outside of the active wedge area.
